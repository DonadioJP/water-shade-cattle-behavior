Key Findings

An integrated interpretation of thermal environment, behavioral responses, and water balance

Note

This page synthesizes results from the Thermal Comfort and Behavior pages. Every value is computed from the models on those pages when the site is rendered. Model results are estimated marginal means (EMMs) from mixed models with group × period (16 units) as the experimental unit; behavioral percentages are raw means across animals (Table 2 of the Behavior page).

1 · Shade Reduced Thermal Load — But Did Not Eliminate Heat Stress

Shade access reduced black globe temperature by 4.22 °C on average (32.66 °C in sun vs 28.44 °C in shade; p < 0.001), with peak unshaded temperatures reaching 50.7 °C. Radiant thermal load was 21.1% lower under shade (520.4 vs 659.2 W/m²; p < 0.001).

Despite this reduction, thermal stress was not fully eliminated in either condition. Using BGHI thresholds (Baeta & Souza, 2010; comfort ≤ 74, alert > 74–78, danger > 78–84, critical > 84):

Condition Critical hours (BGHI > 84) Danger or critical hours (BGHI > 78) Hours recorded
Sun 34 56 88
Shade 10 47 88

Shade reduced critical-threshold hours by 71%, but shaded paddocks still reached danger or critical levels in 47 of 88 recorded hours. Shade alone did not remove heat stress under the conditions observed.

Soil surface temperature followed the same gradient — shade 25.32 °C, sun between trees 30.23 °C, full sun 32.06 °C (shade vs sun between trees: p < 0.001; shade vs full sun: p < 0.001) — so the benefit of shade extended to the ground where cattle lie.

2 · Daytime Behavior: Water Restriction Reduced Grazing; Shade Helped Only With Water

Treatment affected daytime grazing (p = 0.002), other behaviors (p < 0.001) and standing (p = 0.001), but not lying (p = 0.481) and ruminating (p = 0.155). In the shade × water factorial models, water access had the clearest effect on daytime grazing (p < 0.001). Shade and water interacted (p = 0.033): shade increased grazing when water was ad libitum (S+W+ vs S−W+: p = 0.014) and had no effect under water restriction (S+W− vs S−W−: p = 0.658).

S+W+ animals grazed the most (76.8% of daytime scans) and stood the least (7.4%). Daytime standing rose with both lack of shade (factorial shade p = 0.005) and water restriction (water p < 0.001), reaching 18.5% of scans in S−W−.

S+W− as the pivotal treatment. Animals with shade but restricted water (S+W−) grazed 66.9% of daytime scans, against 76.8% in S+W+ and 67.6% in S−W−. Under water restriction, shade did not change daytime grazing, so the thermal comfort provided by shade did not offset the effect of restricted water.

3 · Nocturnal Grazing Was Highest in S+W−

At night, treatment affected grazing (p < 0.001) and lying (p = 0.010), but not other behaviors (p = 0.077), ruminating (p = 0.989) and standing (p = 0.174). The highest nocturnal grazing was in S+W− (15.6% of night scans). In the factorial model the shade × water interaction was p = 0.094, and shade increased nocturnal grazing under water restriction (S+W− vs S−W−: p < 0.001). This is consistent with S+W− animals recovering at night some of the grazing they lost during the day. Water access on its own did not predict nocturnal grazing (p = 0.274).

4 · Water Restriction Changed Drinking Frequency More Than Intake

Counting every animal-day, W+ animals drank 1.8× as often as W− animals (3.14 vs 1.75 events/animal/day; p < 0.001). Daily intake was 22.6 L/animal in W+ and 19.1 L in W− (p = 0.173 in the mixed model; p = 0.047 on group × period means), so the 3.5 L/day gap was not significant in the mixed model.

Within individual drinking bouts:

Variable W+ W− p
Duration (s, geometric mean) 17.8 21.1 0.141
Sips per event 15.5 18.8 0.047
Sip rate (sips/s) 0.832 0.82 0.811

Restricted animals took more sips per bout, with bouts that were not significantly longer, and drank at the same sip rate. Any compensation therefore came from the number of sips per visit, not from drinking faster.

5 · Water-Restricted Animals Used Shade More

Among S+ treatments, animals with restricted water (S+W−) spent a significantly greater proportion of daytime scans in shade than ad libitum animals (59.6% vs 54.7%; p < 0.001). The difference came mainly from the morning (07:00–11:00h: 10.5 percentage points more shade in S+W−), when W− animals could not drink. The 12:00h and 17:00h scan blocks were excluded from this comparison because S+W− animals were in the trough corridor at those times.

6 · Body Surface Temperature: Shade Mattered, Water Did Not

Flank surface temperature (right and left flank averaged) was 36.29 ± 0.44 °C in S+W+ and 36.43 ± 0.44 °C in S+W−, against 38.37 ± 0.44 °C in S−W+ and 38.67 ± 0.44 °C in S−W− (EMM ± SE; treatment p = 0.006). In the factorial model, the shade effect had p = 0.001 and the water effect p = 0.484 (interaction p = 0.788).

Time of day had a large effect: flank temperature was 39.16 ± 0.40 °C at 10:00h, 37.83 ± 0.40 °C at 13:00h and 35.32 ± 0.40 °C at 16:00h (p < 0.001).

Coat color affected surface temperature when tested on one mean per animal (n = 16; F(2, 13) = 6.21, p = 0.013): black 37.92 ± 0.36 °C, red 37.42 ± 0.34 °C and cream 36.69 ± 0.41 °C (EMM ± SE from the mixed model; black vs cream p = 0.010, black vs red p = 0.204). Breed had no detectable effect (F(1, 14) = 0.01, p = 0.919). All red animals were Braford and all black and cream animals were Jersey, so coat and breed effects cannot be fully separated.

7 · Integrated Interpretation

Shade lowered the thermal load and the animals’ body surface temperature, and it supported daytime grazing — but only when water was freely available. Water restriction reduced daytime grazing, raised daytime standing, cut drinking frequency, and increased the use of shade in the morning. S+W− animals grazed most at night, which suggests they recovered part of the daytime grazing they lost.

Together, these results indicate that shade and water act through different but interacting pathways: shade changes the thermal microenvironment, while water availability limits how much of that benefit the animals can turn into grazing. Neither resource alone was enough to fully support behavior under the conditions observed.

Experimental unit. Treatments were applied to 4 groups over 4 periods, so there are only 16 experimental units. When every outcome was analysed on group × period means (Latin square, 6 error df), 51 of 55 tests agreed with the mixed models at the 0.05 level (Behavior page, Table 47a).

## 6 · Body Surface Temperature: Shade Mattered, Water Did Not

Flank surface temperature (right and left flank averaged) was **`r TH$bt_spwp` °C** in S+W+ and **`r TH$bt_spwm` °C** in S+W−, against **`r TH$bt_smwp` °C** in S−W+ and **`r TH$bt_smwm` °C** in S−W− (EMM ± SE; treatment *p* `r TH$thermo_trt_p_text`). In the factorial model, the shade effect had *p* `r TH$thermo_fact_shade_p` and the water effect *p* `r TH$thermo_fact_water_p` (interaction *p* `r TH$thermo_fact_int_p`).

Time of day had a large effect: flank temperature was `r TH$bt_10` °C at 10:00h, `r TH$bt_13` °C at 13:00h and `r TH$bt_16` °C at 16:00h (*p* `r TH$thermo_clock_p_text`).

Coat color `r if (TH$coat_animal_sig) "affected" else "did not clearly affect"` surface temperature when tested on one mean per animal (n = `r nrow(TH$animal_means)`; `r TH$coat_animal_f`): black `r TH$bt_black` °C, red `r TH$bt_red` °C and cream `r TH$bt_cream` °C (EMM ± SE from the mixed model; black vs cream *p* `r TH$coat_animal_p_bc`, black vs red *p* `r TH$coat_animal_p_br`). Breed `r if (TH$breed_animal_aov["breed", "Pr(>F)"] < 0.05) "also had an effect" else "had no detectable effect"` (`r TH$breed_animal_f`). All red animals were Braford and all black and cream animals were Jersey, so coat and breed effects cannot be fully separated.
