Effect of the combination of water and shade on the behavior and thermal comfort of cattle in a pasture system

Abstract

The availability of shade and water is essential in any animal husbandry system, as they directly interfere with animal behavior, productivity and well-being. However, extrinsic factors such as social dominance and climatic conditions affect voluntary water intake, causing behavioral and physiological changes with negative consequences for the health and welfare of animals. In the present work, the effect of water supply and shade availability on the behavior and thermal comfort of cattle in Pastoreio Racional Voisin (PRV) was evaluated, with the aim of improving the supply of natural resources to ensure a more resilient breeding system for the animals.

Thirty-two females of the Braford breed (478 ± 44 kg of body weight) and Jersey (385 ± 33 kg of body weight) participated in the experiment, divided into four groups with eight animals each (four Braford and four Jersey cows), in a 4 × 4 Latin square design with one rest day, totaling 20 days of experiment. Four treatments were applied: T1: shade + 24h water (SAV); T2: shade + restricted water supply from 12:00–12:30 and 17:30–18:00 (SAL); T3: no shade + 24h water (RAV); and T4: no shade + restricted water from 12:00–12:30 and 17:30–18:00 (RAL).

The availability of water and shade influenced the behavior and thermal comfort of the cows. During the day, cows in the SAV treatment spent more time grazing and ruminating while lying down, and had less idle standing time (78.8%, 11.2% and 4.2% respectively; p < 0.05). Cows in the RAL and SAL treatments spent less time grazing (67.7% and 66.9%) and more time standing idle (10.9% for both; p < 0.05). During the night, lying rumination was the most prevalent behavior across all groups, but the SAL group spent 15.6% of time grazing — approximately 3% more than the other groups. Animals from the RAL and SAL treatments showed higher average drinking time (41.4 and 36.9 s/event) and greater number of sips per event (19.9 and 19.0, respectively). The RAV treatment had the highest sip rate (0.55/s) while SAV had the lowest (0.48/s); however, water consumption was equivalent among RAL, RAV and SAV, differing only from SAL, which had lower consumption (135.8 L).

Shade contributed to the reduction of the Black Globe Temperature and Humidity Index (ITGU): in shaded treatments, mean values did not exceed 78 (alert level), while unshaded treatments reached values >84 (critical level) for 6 hours. Mean soil surface temperatures were 32.1 ± 7.64 °C in RAL and RAV, and 25.3 ± 3.17 °C in shaded areas (SAV and SAL). Animals in the RAL treatment showed higher body surface temperatures (38.69 °C at the right flank) compared to SAV animals (36.32 °C).

The availability of shade and water increased resting and grazing time, reduced the number of sips per drinking event and total drinking time, and favored more stable body surface temperatures — outcomes with direct implications for animal welfare and productivity.

1 Introduction

[Introduction to be written — see notes below for structure]

The hypothesis of the study was that the availability of water and shade alters the ingestive behavior of cattle, resulting in greater rest time during the hottest periods of the day compared to restricted or no access to these resources. However, during the night, cattle raised with free access to water and shade show greater grazing time, compensating for the reduction of this behavior during the day.

The objectives of the study were:

  1. To evaluate the effect of ad libitum water availability on the diurnal and nocturnal behavior of cows;
  2. To evaluate the effect of shade provision on the diurnal and nocturnal behavior of cows;
  3. To evaluate the effect of ad libitum water availability, combined or not with shade, on the thermal comfort of cows.

2 Methods

Animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee on Animal Use (CEUA) of the Federal University of Santa Catarina (UFSC) (CEUA Protocol 857122042).

2.1 Study Site and Animals

The study was conducted at the Bovine Vivarium of the Ressacada Experimental Farm (FER) — Federal University of Santa Catarina, located in Florianópolis, Brazil (27°40’25” S; 48°32’30” W), from December 2021 to April 2022. The experimental area covered 3.2 ha and was divided into twelve paddocks of 2,600 m² each. Six paddocks were shaded by Inga edulis trees planted in rows with 3 m spacing between plants and 20 m between rows. The remaining six paddocks contained pasture only. Forage species present across all paddocks included plants from the genera Brachiaria, Melilotus, Setaria, Axonopus, Cynodon, Panicum, Hemarthria, Desmodium, Trifolium, Lotus, Pennisetum, Arachis, Stylosanthes, Lolium and Paspalum.

2.2 Experimental Design

Thirty-two females of the Braford (mean body weight: 478 ± 44 kg) and Jersey (mean body weight: 385 ± 33 kg) breeds were divided into four groups of eight animals each (four Braford and four Jersey per group). Animals were individually identified with ear tags and numbered on both flanks using non-toxic paint. The experiment was conducted in a 4 × 4 Latin square design over four periods (repetitions), with groups randomly allocated to treatments in each period. Treatments were:

Animals were weighed individually at the start and end of each period. Each repetition occurred at 30-day intervals and lasted five days: two days of adaptation, two days of data collection, separated by one rest day. Groups were managed under the Voisin Rational Grazing system [@MachadoFilho2021]. Paddocks (2,600 m²) were subdivided with temporary fencing (1,300 m²), yielding 162 m² of pasture per animal. Animals remained 24h in each subdivision and were moved daily at 06:50h. In shaded treatments, each subdivision included 390 m² of shade area, guaranteeing 48.7 m²/animal.

2.3 Data Collection

Behavioral assessments occurred from 07:00 on one day to 07:00 the following day, on alternate days, totaling 48h of observation per period. Observers underwent training and two reliability tests. During data collection, observers alternated between treatments and animal groups to reduce observational bias. Both reliability tests achieved 95% agreement between observers [@Lehner1996].

Observers were positioned at a distance that allowed observation without interfering with animal behavior. Short-duration behaviors were recorded as events; longer-duration behaviors were classified as states and sampled at regular intervals as instantaneous records [@Altmann1974].

2.3.1 Behavioral Observations

Individual behavioral observations were recorded on field sheets simultaneously across all four groups, noting the animal, paddock location and time. Records were taken instantaneously every 10 minutes, according to Table 2. The ethogram follows definitions adopted by the Laboratory of Applied Ethology (LETA) [@Coimbra2012; @Bica2020; @Sousa2021].

2.3.2 Water Consumption

Water meters (Unimag®, precision 0.01 L) were installed at the inlet of each trough — between the ball valve and flange — to record consumption per group individually. Troughs were circular plastic reservoirs (Multilit®, 500 L; 0.6 m height × 1.2 m diameter), marked at the upper rim to indicate maximum water level. Consumption was recorded on all observation days at 07:00 and 19:00h. Volume differences were corrected for daily precipitation and total evaporation relative to trough surface area. All troughs were supplied from the same water source, controlled by a float valve.

In the SAL and RAL treatments, animals were led through a corridor (5 m wide) to troughs located 100 m from the paddocks at the designated times (12:00–12:30 and 17:30–18:00). These drinking times reflect practices commonly observed on farms that do not provide in-paddock water. In SAV and RAV treatments, troughs remained available throughout the day.

To reduce human interference with animal behavior and facilitate nighttime observation of drinking events, surveillance cameras (Intelbras®) were installed at each trough, connected to a video storage device, recording continuously during observation days.

Drinking behavior was evaluated as events [@Coimbra2012]: at each trough visit, time, number of sips (G), animal identity (AE), drinking time (TB) and sip rate (TG, sips/second) were recorded. Water intake was considered when the animal had its lips submerged and was performing swallowing movements. Time spent with head raised was discounted and restarted when the animal resumed drinking during the same visit.

2.4 Thermal Comfort

Climatic variables — air temperature (TA), relative humidity (RH), wind speed, evaporation, rainfall and sunshine hours — were collected daily at the FER–UFSC Meteorological Station (station ID 1036), located 300 m from the experimental area.

The Black Globe Temperature and Humidity Index (ITGU) was calculated hourly from 09:00 to 18:00h as an indicator of heat stress in cattle. Black globe thermometers were installed in shaded and unshaded paddocks, fixed at 1 m height above ground (approximately the trunk height of a medium-sized cow). Globe temperature (TGN) data were recorded every 5 minutes from 09:00 to 18:00h using a Data Logger (Elitech® RC-4HC).

ITGU was calculated using Equation 1 described by @Buffington1981:

\[ \text{ITGU} = T_{gn} + 0.36 \cdot T_{po} - 41.5 \tag{1}\]

Where: ITGU = Black Globe Temperature and Humidity Index; \(T_{gn}\) = black globe temperature (°C); \(T_{po}\) = dew point temperature (°C).

Dew point temperature (TPO) was calculated using Equation 2 simplified by @Wilhelm1976:

\[ T_{po} = T_a - \frac{(100 - \text{RH})}{5} \tag{2}\]

Where: \(T_a\) = air temperature (°C); RH = relative humidity (%).

ITGU thresholds follow @Baeta2010: ≤74 = comfort; 75–78 = alert; 79–84 = danger; >84 = critical.

The Radiant Thermal Load (CTR) was calculated using Equation 3 proposed by @Esmay1982:

\[ \text{CTR} = \sigma \times T_m^4 \tag{3}\]

Where: \(\sigma\) = Stefan-Boltzmann constant (5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/m² K⁴); \(T_m\) = mean radiant temperature (W/m²).

Soil surface temperature (TS) was measured hourly from 09:00 to 18:00h in all treatments using an infrared thermometer (Simpla® TI-55; range −55 to 550 °C; precision ±1 °C; resolution 0.1 °C). In RAL and RAV, measurements were taken at four random points; in SAV and SAL, at eight points (four under tree canopy, four in unshaded areas). All measurements were performed by the same person following a standardized positioning and distance protocol.

For non-invasive assessment of treatment effects on animal body surface temperature, thermographic images were obtained from 16 cows (four per group: two Braford and two Jersey). The anatomical regions assessed were: eye (O), right flank (FD), left flank (FE) and posterior udder (U) [@Montanholi2008; @Sorensen2014]. Animals were photographed at 3 m distance at 10:00, 13:00 and 16:00h during collection days in each period (with the exception of the last day of period 4 due to rain). Images were captured using an infrared thermographic camera (Flir® T640; emissivity coefficient 0.98 for biological tissues, per manufacturer recommendation) and analyzed in Flir Thermal Studio® software to extract minimum, mean and maximum temperatures for each anatomical region. Mean and maximum temperatures were used for analysis; minimum values were excluded due to greater environmental influence [@Montanholi2008; @Salles2016].

2.5 Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed in RStudio (R Core Team, 2019). Descriptive analyses (frequency, mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum) were based on treatment-level data, since all groups were observed for the same period (24h/day over eight days). Data normality was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test at 95% confidence.

2.5.1 Behavioral Analysis

Cow behavior was compared across treatments (RAL, RAV, SAL, SAV). The experimental design consisted of two repetitions (days) per four periods, four independent variables (treatments) and dependent variables (behaviors in Table 2 plus drinking behaviors: TB, G, TG, CH) measured over 24h test days. Data were submitted to influence analysis using a Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLM) with Gaussian or Poisson distribution (depending on the distribution of the response variable) at 95% confidence. Animals, days and hours were defined as random effects, following the model:

\[ Y_{ij} = \alpha_j + A_i + B_i + \beta_{ij} + e_{ij} \]

Where: \(Y_{ij}\) = measured behavior; \(\alpha_j\) = fixed effects (treatments); \(A_i\) = random effect of individual animals; \(B_i\) = random effect of breed; \(\beta_{ij}\) = random effect of days × hours; \(e_{ij}\) = residual error.

To evaluate treatment effects on water consumption (L), a mixed GLM was applied:

\[ Y_{ij} = \alpha_j + \beta_{ij} + e_{ij} \]

Where: \(Y_i\) = water consumption; \(\alpha_j\) = fixed effects (treatments); \(\beta_i\) = random effect; \(i\) = days; \(j\) = hours; \(e_{ij}\) = residual error.

2.5.2 Microclimatic and Thermal Comfort Analysis

Analysis was conducted in two stages: (1) across treatments (SAL, SAV, RAV, RAL) and (2) between shaded vs. unshaded conditions. For both analyses, the experimental design consisted of two repetitions per period (4), four independent variables and five dependent variables (TA, RH, TS, TGN, ITGU) measured from 08:00 to 19:00h. Data were submitted to influence analysis using a mixed GLM at 95% confidence:

\[ Y_{ij} = \alpha_j + \beta_{ij} + e_{ij} \]

Where: \(Y_{ij}\) = microclimatic variable or thermal comfort index; \(\alpha_j\) = fixed effects (treatments or conditions); \(\beta_{ij}\) = random effect; \(i\) = days; \(j\) = hours; \(e_{ij}\) = residual error.

Body surface temperatures (O, U, FD, FE) were treated as dependent variables with treatments as independent variables, analyzed using a mixed GLM with Gaussian or Poisson distribution at 95% confidence. Animals, breed, days and hours were defined as random effects:

\[ Y_{ij} = \alpha_j + A_i + B_i + \beta_{ij} + e_{ij} \]

Where: \(Y_{ij}\) = body surface temperature; \(\alpha_j\) = fixed effects (treatments); \(A_i\) = random effect of individual animals; \(B_i\) = random effect of breed; \(\beta_{ij}\) = random effect; \(i\) = days; \(j\) = hours; \(e_{ij}\) = residual error.

All analyses were performed separately for each variable. Soil surface temperature, black globe temperature and animal surface temperature were submitted to regression analyses between each other at 95% confidence. Meteorological station data (station ID 1036) were used for general descriptive analysis (24h).
